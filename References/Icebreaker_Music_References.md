# References on Music and Stress Reduction

## 1. de Witte et al. (2020)

*Effects of music interventions on stress-related outcomes: a systematic review and two meta-analyses*

**Journal:** Health Psychology Review, 14(2), 294–324

**Link:** [https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2019.1627897](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2019.1627897)

**Summary:** A large-scale meta-analysis combining 104 randomized controlled trials, 327 effect sizes, and 9,617 participants. Overall, music interventions showed significant improvements in both physiological stress markers (heart rate, blood pressure, hormone levels) and psychological stress outcomes (restlessness, anxiety, nervousness), with the strongest effect seen on heart rate. This is one of the most-cited reviews in the field (85+ citations).

## 2. de Witte et al. (2022)

*Music therapy for stress reduction: a systematic review and meta-analysis*

**Journal:** Health Psychology Review, 16(1), 134–159

**Link:** [https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2020.1846580](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2020.1846580)

**Summary:** Unlike the previous paper, this one focuses specifically on "music therapy" — personalized interventions led by a trained music therapist — rather than casual music listening. A multilevel meta-analysis of 47 studies and 2,747 participants found a medium-to-large effect size (d = .723) on stress-related outcomes, stronger than the effect of music listening alone. This suggests that who designs the music experience matters — worth keeping in mind when designing the soundtrack for your ice-breaker card game, since simply playing any track isn't guaranteed to work.

## 3. Adiasto et al. (2022)

*Music listening and stress recovery in healthy individuals: A systematic review with meta-analysis of experimental studies*

**Journal:** PLOS ONE, 17(6), e0270031

**Link:** [https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0270031](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0270031)

**Summary:** I deliberately included this one to push back a bit — it's important not to only present evidence that's convenient. Across 14 experiments and 706 healthy participants who were first put through a lab-induced stressor and then randomly assigned to music or a control condition, the cumulative effect of music on stress recovery was not statistically significant. The authors note that effects varied a lot depending on genre, who selected the music, and tempo — suggesting that random playback isn't necessarily effective; song choice and context matter more than simply "having music or not."

## 4. Thoma et al. (2013)

*The Effect of Music on the Human Stress Response*

**Journal:** PLoS ONE, 8(8), e70156

**Link:** [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23940541/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23940541/)

**Summary:** A classic physiological experiment. Sixty healthy women were randomly assigned to listen to relaxing music (Allegri's "Miserere"), the sound of rippling water, or silence before a standardized psychosocial stress test, while salivary cortisol, salivary alpha-amylase, and heart rate were repeatedly measured. Cortisol responses differed significantly across the three groups, and alpha-amylase returned to baseline faster in the music group — but heart rate and self-reported stress showed no significant differences between groups. This suggests music's effect may show up more clearly in the autonomic nervous system than in subjective experience.

## 5. Baltazar et al. (2019)

*Is it me or the music? Stress reduction and the role of regulation strategies and music*

**Journal:** Music & Science, 2 (Sage)

**Link:** [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2059204319844161](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2059204319844161)

**Summary:** This study examines music as a tool for emotion regulation, separating out the individual contributions of "regulation strategy" versus "the music itself" to stress reduction. 34 participants first self-reported which music and which coping strategies felt "adequate" or "inadequate" for them personally, then went through a lab-induced stressor before trying different combinations to calm down. The key takeaway is individual variation — the same song affects different people differently, and music the user themselves finds effective tends to outperform music arbitrarily assigned by researchers.
