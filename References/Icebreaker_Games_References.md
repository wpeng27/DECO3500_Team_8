# References on Icebreaker Games and Low-Pressure Social Interaction

## 1. Bali et al. (2023)

*Nooks: Social spaces to lower hesitations in interacting with new people at work*

**Conference:** Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems. Association for Computing Machinery

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580796](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580796)

**Summary:** Nooks is a Slack-based system that hides the creator of a conversation topic and brings together people interested in that topic, reducing uncertainty and fear of social evaluation. A deployment in a summer research program supported new online and offline interactions. For icebreaker design, this suggests that shared interests and reduced personal exposure can help people initiate conversations, although the study examined a social platform rather than a game.

## 2. Chiarandini & Sun (2024)

*Team formation with diversity and similarity goals*

**Conference:** Proceedings of the 14th International Conference on the Practice and Theory of Automated Timetabling (PATAT 2024), 367–376

**Link:** [https://patatconference.org/patat2024/proceedings/papers/367.pdf](https://patatconference.org/patat2024/proceedings/papers/367.pdf)

**Summary:** The authors present a team-formation tool that controls diversity or similarity within teams while promoting comparable team compositions across groups. Its practical contribution is a flexible method for forming balanced student teams. This can inform grouping before an icebreaker, but the paper does not directly evaluate social pressure or icebreaker effectiveness.

## 3. Depping & Mandryk (2017)

*Cooperation and interdependence: How multiplayer games increase social closeness*

**Conference:** Proceedings of the Annual Symposium on Computer-Human Interaction in Play, 449–461. Association for Computing Machinery

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1145/3116595.3116639](https://doi.org/10.1145/3116595.3116639)

**Summary:** Using four versions of an online game played by pairs of strangers, the study found that cooperation and interdependence independently supported social bonds and improved play experiences. Increased conversational turns helped explain the social-closeness benefit of interdependence. This supports designing icebreakers with a shared goal and reasons to communicate, although social closeness is not the same outcome as reduced social pressure.

## 4. Depping et al. (2016)

*Trust me: Social games are better than social icebreakers at building trust*

**Conference:** Proceedings of the 2016 Annual Symposium on Computer-Human Interaction in Play, 116–129. Association for Computing Machinery

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1145/2967934.2968097](https://doi.org/10.1145/2967934.2968097)

**Summary:** An online experiment found that a cooperative multiplayer game built more interpersonal trust than a conversation-based social icebreaker, while also supporting relational communication. The findings support shared activities as an alternative to directly exchanging personal information. However, the experiment involved pairs playing one game, so it does not establish that every game will reduce pressure or work equally well in larger groups.

## 5. Desai et al. (2021)

*Stress-reducing effects of playing a casual video game among undergraduate students*

**Journal:** Trends in Psychology, 29(3), 563–579

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1007/s43076-021-00062-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43076-021-00062-6)

**Summary:** In a study of 80 undergraduates, both 20 minutes of playing Flower and a body-scan mindfulness session were followed by reductions in psychological stress, heart rate, and blood pressure. Mindfulness produced a greater reduction in self-reported psychological stress, while physiological reductions were similar between interventions. This provides indirect support for relaxing casual gameplay, but the study did not evaluate social icebreakers or interaction between strangers.

## 6. Fu et al. (2025)

*Group sports games for social anxiety in university students: A randomized controlled trial revealing gender-specific efficacy*

**Journal:** BMC Psychology, 13, Article 1420

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-03756-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-03756-z)

**Summary:** This randomized trial assigned 105 university students with elevated social anxiety to therapeutic group sports games or aerobic exercise for ten weeks. The games programme produced greater reductions in interaction anxiety and fear of negative evaluation among female participants; male participants improved in both groups without a significant difference between interventions. Its structured social activities suggest a possible rationale for supportive group play, although a ten-week therapeutic programme does not establish the effectiveness of a brief icebreaker.

## 7. Jarusriboonchai et al. (2015)

*CueSense: A wearable proximity-aware display enhancing encounters*

**Conference:** Proceedings of the 33rd Annual ACM Conference Extended Abstracts on Human Factors in Computing Systems. Association for Computing Machinery

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1145/2702613.2732833](https://doi.org/10.1145/2702613.2732833)

**Summary:** CueSense displays information from wearers' social-media profiles, selecting content according to proximity and similarities between users' profiles to provide potential conversation cues. A preliminary study with 18 participants explored how visible, socially relevant information might help initiate first encounters. It offers a design example of external conversation prompts, but its exploratory evidence does not establish that such prompts reduce social anxiety.

## 8. Kankainen & Paavilainen (2019)

*Hybrid board game design guidelines*

**Conference:** Proceedings of DiGRA 2019 Conference: Game, Play and the Emerging Ludo-Mix. Digital Games Research Association

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.26503/dl.v2019i1.1098](https://doi.org/10.26503/dl.v2019i1.1098)

**Summary:** The authors present 17 hybrid board-game design guidelines developed through workshops with researchers and industry experts, developer interviews, and a player survey. Guidelines on accessibility, tutorials, and sociability recommend making games easy to enter and learn while using digital elements to support communication and shared play. These principles can inform accessible icebreaker design, although the paper presents design guidance rather than a direct evaluation of stress reduction.

## 9. Kleinschmit et al. (2025)

*A group-forming course content-focused icebreaker for life science classrooms*

**Journal:** Journal of Microbiology & Biology Education, 26(1), Article e00173-24

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00173-24](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00173-24)

**Summary:** Students receive illustrated course-content tickets and form groups by discussing common themes, giving them a shared task and some autonomy over group formation. Feedback and engagement data from implementation at three institutions were largely positive, with students reporting that the activity helped build a community of learners. The activity provides an alternative to icebreakers centred on personal disclosure, but the reported feedback does not establish a measured reduction in anxiety.

## 10. McPherson et al. (2001)

*Birds of a feather: Homophily in social networks*

**Journal:** Annual Review of Sociology, 27, 415–444

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.soc.27.1.415](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.soc.27.1.415)

**Summary:** This review describes homophily: social relationships tend to form between people who share characteristics, while relationships between dissimilar people are more likely to dissolve. It also explains that homophily can restrict the information and interactions people encounter. For icebreaker design, discovering shared interests may provide useful conversation openings, although the review does not test games or establish that shared-interest prompts reduce social pressure.

## 11. Pallavicini et al. (2021)

*Commercial off-the-shelf video games for reducing stress and anxiety: Systematic review*

**Journal:** JMIR Mental Health, 8(8), Article e28150

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.2196/28150](https://doi.org/10.2196/28150)

**Summary:** This systematic review included 28 studies and found evidence that commercial video games can reduce stress and anxiety, including benefits from some short play sessions. Outcomes varied across games, populations, and methods, and some studies reported increased stress. For icebreaker design, the findings support exploring brief, enjoyable play as a relaxing activity, but they do not directly establish that a social icebreaker game will reduce anxiety.

## 12. Reinecke (2009)

*Games and recovery: The use of video and computer games to recuperate from stress and strain*

**Journal:** Journal of Media Psychology, 21(3), 126–142

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-1105.21.3.126](https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-1105.21.3.126)

**Summary:** An online survey of 1,614 participants found that people used video and computer games after stressful or exhausting situations, and stronger perceived recovery experiences were associated with more frequent game use for recovery. Work-related fatigue and daily hassles were also associated with this use. The findings provide a rationale for considering games as restorative activities, but the survey cannot establish causation and does not directly examine pressure during group icebreakers.

## 13. Van Bogart et al. (2023)

*Iterative design, feasibility, and preliminary efficacy testing for the development of a cooperative card game intervention to reduce loneliness and foster social connection*

**Journal:** Games for Health Journal, 12(5), 377–384

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1089/g4h.2022.0245](https://doi.org/10.1089/g4h.2022.0245)

**Summary:** The authors developed Connections, a cooperative card game informed by research on self-disclosure, interpersonal closeness, and serious games. Pilot participants found it enjoyable and helpful for connection, and reported lower loneliness, depressed mood, and anxiousness alongside greater willingness to connect and open up. This offers preliminary support for cooperative conversation games, although larger samples and controlled trials are needed before firm conclusions about efficacy.

## 14. Xu et al. (2011)

*Chores are fun: Understanding social play in board games for digital tabletop game design*

**Conference:** Proceedings of DiGRA 2011 Conference: Think Design Play. Digital Games Research Association

**Link:** [https://doi.org/10.26503/dl.v2011i1.591](https://doi.org/10.26503/dl.v2011i1.591)

**Summary:** Through video analysis of board-game sessions, the authors identified five categories of social interaction and argued that routine game activities, such as learning rules, waiting for turns, and moving pieces, create opportunities for engagement. These activities supported discussion of strategy, gameplay, the game itself, and topics outside the game. For icebreaker design, this suggests retaining simple shared actions and pauses that invite conversation, although the study does not directly measure anxiety or reduced pressure.
