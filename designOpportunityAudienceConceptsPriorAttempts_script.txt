Through our research, we identified three values that come up again and again: predictability, autonomy, and gradual self-disclosure. Students don't necessarily mind group work itself — what makes it stressful is not knowing who they'll be working with, and feeling like they have to perform socially with strangers from the very first tutorial.

That leads us to our design opportunity: how might we make early group formation more social — through asynchronous, low-pressure introductions — and more contextual, by tying it precisely to the course and tutorial slot a student is actually enrolled in?

Our audience is students in courses with group assessment, especially at the start of semester. This is particularly acute for first-year students, international students, and anyone transferring into a course where they don't already know people. These students are already comfortable using tools like Ed Discussion or course Facebook groups — but almost exclusively for logistics, not for initiating social contact. They're caught in a real tension: they want to find reliable groupmates early, but they're wary of reaching out and being met with silence.

Our concept is a course-specific, time-limited matching forum for UQ students. Rather than one big space per course, spaces are bound to the exact course and tutorial slot a student is in. Students can post anonymously at first and choose when — or whether — to reveal their identity. The forum is only active in the one to two weeks before semester starts, which keeps it focused rather than becoming another dead, low-activity group chat. And once students have matched, they get a simple handoff card with their first tutorial's time and room, easing that transition from "knowing someone online" to actually meeting them.

We looked closely at what already exists. UQ's own Ed Discussion and course Facebook groups cover hundreds of students at once, with no way to narrow down to your specific tutorial group, and they're used almost entirely for logistics rather than social connection. Discord-based study platforms like Study Together are built for co-studying alongside strangers, not for forming an actual assessment group. Swipe-based apps like StudyBuddy ask students to self-expose immediately with a photo and bio, which is exactly the barrier that makes socially anxious students avoid these tools in the first place. And one-to-one platforms like Focusmate solve accountability, not the specific problem of finding compatible groupmates for graded coursework.

What we're proposing sits in a gap none of these fully address: narrow enough to be genuinely relevant — same course, same tutorial, same assessment — and designed from the ground up around lowering the social barrier, not assuming everyone is equally comfortable reaching out first.
