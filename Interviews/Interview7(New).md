### 1. Group Formation Context

**Question:** Think about the most recent time you had to form a course group with classmates you did not know well. What course was it? Were the groups self-formed or assigned?

**Response:**
The most recent time was probably for a design course, where we had to work in a group for a semester-long project. The groups were self-formed, so we had to find our own teammates rather than being randomly assigned.

I knew a couple of people from other courses, but I didn't really know most of the people in the class, so it was a bit awkward trying to figure out who to approach.

### 2. Group Formation Process

**Question:** Could you walk me through what happened, from when the group task was announced to when your group was finally formed?

**Response:**
When the group task was announced, I think everyone immediately started looking around to see who they already knew. There was a bit of a rush because you don't want to be one of the people left without a group.

I initially talked to a couple of people I had spoken to in previous classes. We basically asked each other whether we already had a group and what sort of people we were looking for. From there, we ended up talking to some other classmates who were also looking for members.

There was a bit of back and forth because some people had already made plans with their friends. Eventually, we had enough people and just confirmed the group with everyone.

### 3. Decision-Making

**Question:** How did you decide whom to approach, which group to join, or whom to accept as a group member? What information did you use to make that decision?

**Response:**
The main things I considered were whether I'd interacted with the person before and whether they seemed like someone who would actually contribute.

I wouldn't necessarily judge someone based on whether they were really good at programming or anything like that. For a group project, I'd rather have someone who's reliable and communicates than someone who's really skilled but disappears for two weeks.

I also looked at things like whether they participated in tutorials, whether they seemed engaged in class, and whether they responded when people were talking in the group chat. Those were basically the only signals I had.

### 4. Hesitation and Concerns

**Question:** Was there a moment when you hesitated to contact someone or join a group? If so, what were you concerned about? What did you eventually do?

**Response:**
Yeah, definitely. I remember seeing some people who seemed like they were also looking for a group, but I wasn't sure whether I should approach them.

The main concern was just whether it would be awkward if they said no, or if they already had a group. There's also the thought of, "What if they're not going to do any work?" because you're committing to working with them for a whole semester.

Usually, I just ended up approaching people who looked approachable or who I'd had some interaction with before. Even if I didn't know them properly, having at least spoken once made it much easier.

### 5. Information Needs

**Question:** What would help you judge whether someone might be a reliable or compatible group member? Was there anything you wanted to know but could not notice?

**Response:**
I'd probably want to know how reliable someone is with deadlines, how they communicate, and what their expectations are for the amount of work everyone should do.

It would also be useful to know their strengths. For example, whether they're more interested in programming, UI/UX, documentation, research, or project management.

The problem is that you can't really tell most of that just by looking at someone in a lecture. Someone might seem really quiet but actually be a great teammate, while someone who's really social might not necessarily contribute much to the project.

I think that's probably the biggest issue — you're making quite an important decision based on very limited information.

### 6. Additional Experiences

**Question:** Is there anything important about forming groups with unfamiliar classmates that I have not asked about?

**Response:**
I think there's a social pressure around group formation that people don't really talk about. It can feel like there's a limited window where you're supposed to find a group, and if you don't act quickly, everyone else already has their groups sorted.

It's also harder for people who don't already have a big friend group in the course. Once people start forming groups with their friends, it becomes harder to approach them because you feel like you're interrupting an existing group.

So I think group formation isn't just about finding people with the right skills. It's also about making people feel comfortable approaching each other in the first place.

### 7. Friends Afterwards

**Question:** Have you ever met someone through the group-forming process but not ended up being teammates, yet still stayed in contact or became friends?

**Response:**
Yeah, actually. I've had situations where I talked to someone because we were both looking for a group, but we ended up joining different groups.

We still ended up talking occasionally afterwards, especially if we had other classes together. I think the fact that you already have that initial reason to talk makes it much easier to become friends later.

It's kind of funny because normally you might never speak to someone in a lecture, but once you've had that first conversation, it's not as weird to say hi to them later.

### 8. Course Friends

**Question:** Have you ever wanted to get to know someone from your course but felt unsure about approaching them because you had no reason to talk to them?

**Response:**
Yeah, definitely. There have been plenty of people where I've thought they seemed interesting or like we'd probably get along, but I didn't really have a reason to talk to them.

Especially in large CS courses, you can sit next to the same person for weeks but never actually introduce yourself. You don't want to randomly walk up and say, "Hey, do you want to be friends?" because that would be pretty weird.

Group projects are probably one of the easiest ways to get around that because there's already a natural reason to start a conversation.

### 9. Interaction with Others

**Question:** After a group is formed, do you usually continue interacting with classmates who aren't in your group? Why or why not?

**Response:**
Usually, not that much. Once the group is formed, most of my interaction tends to be with the people in my group because we're working on the same thing.

I'll still talk to other classmates if we're in the same tutorial or if we're working on something outside the group, but there's not really much reason to keep interacting with them otherwise.

I think the group itself can become a bit of a bubble. You're constantly messaging your teammates and meeting them, while everyone else in the course just becomes people you recognise but don't really know.

That said, if there was an easy way to stay connected with other classmates, like seeing what they're interested in or finding people who are working on similar things, I'd probably use it. It would make it easier to interact without having to randomly approach someone.
