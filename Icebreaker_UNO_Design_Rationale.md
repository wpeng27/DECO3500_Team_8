# Olsson et al. (2020): Influence on the Icebreaker Redesign

## Overview

The main influence of Olsson et al. was changing the role of the Icebreaker screen.

Instead of using the screen only to display random UNO rule changes, the redesigned concept uses technology to actively structure and support social interaction between unfamiliar students.

A useful way to understand the three sections is:

- Section 5.1 asks what social effect the technology should create.

- Section 5.2 asks how technology can create that social effect.

- Section 6.2 asks what role technology should take in the social situation.

## Section 5.1 — Social Design Objectives

Section 5.1 focuses on the social outcomes that interactive technologies can be designed to support.

Relevant objectives for Icebreaker include breaking the ice, increasing awareness of other people, engaging people in collective activity and encouraging interaction.

### Breaking the Ice

The paper discusses technology that can support new encounters by reducing tension and awkwardness when unfamiliar people begin interacting.

> “relieve tension, alleviate social awkwardness”

This influenced Icebreaker to avoid immediately asking students to reveal personal information.

Instead, the first interaction is deliberately light and focused on the UNO game.

For example, Stage 1 — Colour Call asks everyone to respond to the UNO game together before anyone is required to directly interact with another person.

### Increasing Awareness

Olsson et al. identify increasing awareness as an important social design objective.

Technology can help people become more aware of other participants and their actions.

This influenced Stage 2 — Support Move.

Rather than concentrating only on their own cards, a player is prompted to notice another person's situation and briefly interact with them.

For example, the screen might ask a player to ask another participant what colour would help them.

### Collective Activity

The paper discusses activities where multiple people need to work together rather than act independently.

> “joint activity that requires collaboration”

This strongly influenced Stage 4 — Team Round.

Normal UNO is competitive, but the screen temporarily replaces individual winning with a shared goal.

For example, the table may be asked to get every player's hand down to five cards or fewer before a timer expires.

### Encouraging Interaction

Olsson et al. distinguish between simply allowing interaction and providing people with an active reason to interact.

> “reasons and motivations for interaction”

This influenced Stage 3 — Partner Decision.

Instead of hoping that two unfamiliar students naturally begin talking, the screen temporarily pairs them and gives them a short game-related reason to communicate.

## Section 5.2 — Design Approaches

Section 5.2 focuses on the mechanisms designers can use to achieve the social objectives described in Section 5.1.

### 5.2.1 — Shared Digital Workspace

Olsson et al. discuss shared screens and displays that provide participants with a common frame of reference.

These shared displays can also help participants remain aware of what the group is doing.

> “shared attention”

This influenced the redesign of the Icebreaker screen from a simple rule display into a shared coordination space.

For example, during Team Round the screen can display the shared goal, timer and progress of all players.

This gives the table one shared object to look at and respond to.

### 5.2.3 — Introducing Constraints

One particularly important approach discussed by Olsson et al. is the use of technological constraints that make people depend on each other.

> “asymmetric abilities and asymmetric information”

This influenced Stage 5 — Interdependence Round.

Instead of every player having the same responsibility, the system gives different participants temporary roles.

- The Guide provides strategic suggestions.

- The Rule Keeper explains the current challenge.

- The Supporter provides one special support action.

- The Coordinator keeps track of the shared objective.

Because players have different responsibilities, interaction becomes useful to completing the challenge rather than being added only as conversation.

### 5.2.5 — Open Space for Shared Activity

The paper also discusses shared activities that people can observe and join without being heavily pressured.

> “without feeling pressured”

This influenced the decision to keep digital interventions short and return regularly to normal UNO.

The intended rhythm is normal UNO, followed by a short social intervention, and then a return to normal UNO.

The technology should support the social interaction rather than dominate it.

## Section 6.2 — Roles of Enhancement Technology

Section 6.2 brings the previous ideas together by describing different roles technology can take in face-to-face social interaction.

The four important roles are Enable, Facilitate, Invite and Encourage.

### Enable

Enabling technology simply makes an activity possible.

The earlier Icebreaker prototype mainly operated at this level.

The screen displayed a rule and players followed it.

This explains the tutor's criticism that the screen could be replaced by drawing a paper rule from a hat.

### Facilitate

Facilitating technology makes communication or collaboration easier.

Icebreaker now uses the screen to structure interactions so that students do not have to independently decide when or how to start talking.

For example, Support Move provides a specific game-related reason to address another player.

### Invite

Inviting technology exposes an opportunity for social interaction while still leaving participants with some choice.

In Icebreaker, a short temporary partner activity creates an opportunity for two unfamiliar students to work together.

The activity does not suggest that they must become assignment teammates.

### Encourage

Encouraging technologies go beyond simply showing opportunities and actively nudge people toward interaction.

> “subtle intervention”

This was one of the strongest influences on the staged interaction design.

The system gradually introduces stronger forms of social interaction.

## How the Paper Influenced the Five Stages

### Stage 1 — Colour Call

**Facilitation and shared attention.**

Everyone performs a simple action together.

### Stage 2 — Support Move

**Awareness and encouraging interaction.**

Players begin noticing and briefly addressing another person.

### Stage 3 — Partner Decision

**Encouragement and structured interaction.**

Two unfamiliar students receive a legitimate reason to communicate.

### Stage 4 — Team Round

**Collective activity and shared workspace.**

The whole table works toward a common objective.

### Stage 5 — Interdependence

**Constraints and asymmetric abilities.**

Different players become useful and dependent on one another.

## Important Design Clarification

- **Olsson et al. do not propose the exact five-stage Icebreaker progression.**

- The sequence of shared attention, awareness, pair interaction, group coordination and interdependence is our design interpretation of the paper.

- The paper provides the theoretical concepts and design approaches.

- The specific UNO challenges and progression are design hypotheses that should be evaluated through user testing.

## Suggested Explanation to the Tutor

Sections 5.1 and 5.2 made us reconsider the screen as more than a rule display.

Section 5.1 showed that technology can aim to break the ice, increase awareness, engage people in collective activity and actively encourage interaction.

Section 5.2 then showed approaches for achieving those goals, particularly shared digital workspaces and interaction constraints such as asymmetric abilities.

Section 6.2 brings these ideas together through the roles of enabling, facilitating, inviting and encouraging interaction.

We therefore changed the screen from simply presenting random UNO rules to progressively facilitating and encouraging interaction.

The screen now uses shared goals, temporary pairings, group progress and asymmetric player roles.

The five-stage progression itself is our own design interpretation of Olsson et al.'s framework.
